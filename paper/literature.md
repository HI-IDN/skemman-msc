# Literature review and framework

The literature behind the [paper brief](brief.md). Tracked in
[#17](https://github.com/HI-IDN/skemman-msc/issues/17).

**Source.** This is a cleaned-up version of a Copilot Researcher response (September 2026).
Every DOI below was checked against Crossref, and each claim is marked with how far it has
been verified. Treat anything marked *unverified* as a lead to check, not a citable fact.

## Core argument

1. **A master's thesis can be a channel for knowledge exchange.** It can bring together a
   problem that originates outside the university, a partner's data and expertise, academic
   methods and supervision, and the student's work. Earlier studies link collaborative
   theses to recruitment, technical knowledge transfer, real problem-solving and long-term
   relationships.
2. **Skemman metadata show visible ties, not their effects.** Named organisations, external
   supervisors, acknowledgements, repeat partners, titles and abstracts can show formal,
   visible ties and how they change over time. They cannot show whether results were
   implemented, the student was hired, or the partnership lasted.
3. **The contribution is specific, not first-of-its-kind.** Swedish census studies, a
   Polish interview study, Finnish case studies and a Norwegian graduate survey are all
   precedents. See [Research gap](#research-gap).

## Conceptual model

The thesis is a time-limited project that spans boundaries and links at least three
parties: the student, the university and an external partner. Knowledge can flow in four
phases:

| Phase | Examples |
|---|---|
| Into the university | real problems, partner data, operational or technical constraints, practitioner expertise |
| Across the boundary | jointly framed research questions, academic and external supervision, shared interpretation, feedback and iteration |
| Out of the university | analyses, models, prototypes, recommendations, graduates who carry skills with them |
| After the project | hiring, continued research collaboration, implementation, publications, patents, repeat partnerships |

What each kind of evidence can support:

| Metadata can show | Metadata can suggest | Metadata cannot show on its own |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis counts and disciplinary mix | Bridging between university and practice | Whether the student was hired |
| Named companies and institutions | Joint framing of the project | Whether results were implemented |
| External supervisors and examiners | Transfer of tacit knowledge | Patents, publications, new products |
| Text signals of external problems or data | Strength and reciprocity of a tie | Measurable impact on the partner |
| Repeat partners | Whether repeat ties are institutional or depend on one supervisor | Later doctoral study |
| Change by year, university, field; topic drift | | |

## Frameworks

| Framework | Strengths here | Limitations here | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knowledge transfer / exchange in university–industry collaboration | Addresses actors, channels and kinds of knowledge flow; covers data, expertise, hiring, networks | Real impact needs data beyond metadata | **Primary** |
| Academic engagement ([Perkmann et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2012.09.007)) | Covers more than patents and commercialisation, including consulting, joint work and informal ties | Mostly about academic staff, not students | Main support |
| Boundary spanning | Treats the student and thesis as the link between two worlds | The quality of bridging is not visible in metadata | **Mechanism** |
| Triple Helix ([Etzkowitz & Leydesdorff 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0048-7333%2899%2900055-4)) | Suits Iceland, where universities, firms and public bodies are closely linked | Too system-level to explain single theses | Context and discussion |
| Mode 2 (Gibbons et al. 1994) | Applied, distributed, interdisciplinary knowledge production | Very broad; hard to measure from metadata | Brief background |
| Work-integrated learning | Links study, employability and real work settings | Shifts the focus to student learning, away from transfer | Secondary |
| Engaged scholarship | Co-production of knowledge between research and practice | Aimed at research practice, not thesis data | Supporting concept |
| CDIO / industry-engaged engineering education | Real projects, professional competence, stakeholders | Better suited to curriculum analysis | Link to the author's earlier teaching work |
| Innovation ecosystems | Helps interpret repeat ties and networks | A network alone does not show mutual value | Possible discussion frame |

**Recommendation.** Use knowledge exchange in university–industry collaboration as the
primary framework, and boundary spanning to explain how a thesis acts as the link.
Recruitment and student mobility then become one channel of transfer. Use Triple Helix only
for context: companies, agencies, hospitals and research institutes in the Icelandic
system. [Ankrah & Al-Tabbaa (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scaman.2015.02.003) classify university–industry collaboration by form,
motive, process and outcome, which maps well onto the coding scale in the brief.

**JEE note.** JEE reviewers will look for an education-research framing. Boundary spanning
and work-integrated learning give the student's role a place in the argument. Knowledge
transfer alone reads as innovation studies.

## Comparable studies

The literature supports an **education-based channel of knowledge transfer** that
conventional technology-transfer metrics (contracts, patents, licences, spin-offs) miss.
Theses, internships and capstone projects can transfer methods, tacit knowledge, networks
and people without any patent or formal contract.

| Study | Country | Design | Key finding | Verification |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Asplund & Bengtsson (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1080/03043797.2019.1604632) | Sweden (Lund, LTH) | All 529 registered engineering MSc theses completed at the Faculty of Engineering LTH in 2016, supervised across 16 departments; follow-up survey of partner firms (139 responses) | 339 of 529 theses were collaborative; firms value recruitment and access to technical knowledge; large regional firms prominent | DOI verified. | [Mamica (2020)](https://doi.org/10.13187/ejced.2020.1.76) | Poland | 150 interviews with students, supervisors and firms | Benefits of theses based on firms' real problems | DOI verified; figures unverified |
| [Kunttu (2017)](https://doi.org/10.22215/timreview/1124) | Finland | Nine long-term university–industry relationships | Student projects and theses as part of relationship-based learning and innovation | DOI verified; figures unverified |

| [Shah & Gillen (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1080/03043797.2023.2253741) | — | Systematic review of university–industry partnerships in engineering education | Capstones, theses, internships and WIL are common forms; barriers are time, supervision, communication, resources | DOI verified |

**Closest precedent.** The Lund studies are the nearest match, and they differ from this one
in a useful way. They look at one year of theses (LTH, 2016) and go deep on the firm side, with a survey
and telephone follow-up. This study covers theses from 2010 to 2026 and looks at the
longitudinal pattern, but cannot see what firms gained.

There is no single reliable Nordic collaboration rate. Sweden gives the best direct
comparison for theses, and Norway the strongest national data on student–workplace
interaction. Denmark and Finland give examples of institutional practice.

## Threats to validity

Repository metadata are often incomplete, inconsistent, variable across years and shaped by
cataloguing practice. Missing author, institution and contributor data can distort network
analyses in particular.

- **Coverage**: not every completed thesis is in Skemman.
- **Changing cataloguing practice**: required fields and templates change.
- **False negatives**: collaboration that is never recorded.
- **False positives**: a company named because of data, prior employment or thanks, without
  any collaboration.
- **Inconsistent names**: abbreviations, Icelandic and English names, mergers and renamings
  create spurious separate partners.
- **Programme changes**: faculty and programme names change.
- **Visibility bias**: formal supervision is easier to see than informal advice.
- **Confidentiality**: sensitive industry projects may be closed or have the partner removed.

Design responses:

- report missingness by year and institution;
- use an explicit partner taxonomy;
- normalise organisation names, keep the raw data and log every cleaning step;
- hand-code a stratified random sample to validate automated classification;
- report narrow and broad definitions of collaboration, and run a sensitivity analysis;
- compare thesis counts with official graduation statistics where available;
- treat text themes as descriptive patterns, not industry sectors;
- avoid causal claims unless other data support them.

The same principle appears in the author's earlier work on teaching: visible process data
are not the same as quality, and counts are a map, not a grade.

## Research gap

Checked so far:

- **Large thesis collections used to measure university–industry collaboration**: yes,
  already done (Lund, 529 and 945 theses). Not a gap.
- **Two universities in the same country compared**: not found among the sources above.
  This needs a targeted search before it is claimed.
- **Long-term change in engineering thesis collaboration**: the closest precedent covers a
  single year (2016). Not found for a period like 2010–2026. Needs a targeted search.
- **Open repository metadata used for this purpose**: not found. Needs a targeted search.

State the gap only as far as the search supports it.

## Link to the author's earlier work

Keep this short in the paper. The author's earlier teaching work argues that real
engineering work develops through iteration, review, documentation and ongoing dialogue
with stakeholders, and that many final projects at HÍ already involve companies, agencies,
research settings and real data. It also separates authentic professional context (open,
real data and reproducible workflows) from direct company involvement.

The common thread: authentic projects can bridge study and work, and theses with an
external partner add an institutional interface where problems, data, expertise and people
move between the university and society. CDIO and authentic assessment are secondary
frameworks. Knowledge exchange remains the primary one.
